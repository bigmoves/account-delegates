Acting for an account

An exploration of the ways several people could act as one atproto account, without sharing its secret. A community, a brand, a newsroom, a band: one DID, one repo, and moderators or staff who legitimately write to it.

Where things stand

A shared password, or app passwords handed around. Every write looks the same on the wire. Nobody can be removed without rotating the secret. Nothing records who did what.

The options

Several ways to do this have been proposed. Each is set out on the same axes, with a diagram, what it keeps, and what it costs. One of them has a running prototype; that is a fact about it, not a verdict.

What the discussion surfaced

Ideas

  • The app holds the account's session. The community signs in to the app once; the app decides which of its users may use that session.
  • Delegated scopes at the person's own server. A person's authorization server grants an app the right to act for other accounts, as new scopes.
  • Sign in as the account. The account's server authenticates the person at their server, checks their standing, and issues the account's session, narrowed.
  • A group host. A dedicated service that is the PDS, the authorization server, and the roles for community accounts, with an admin API for apps.
  • Configuration on the account. The account names who may act for it; its own PDS enforces that on writes, on sign-in, and at creation.

Questions and concerns

  • Consent has to be legible. What does the person see, and does it name an audience? An open audience is a different decision for five friends than for a community of thousands.
  • Delegate writes, not everything. Identity, account, and general calls are the dangerous parts; repo, space, and blob writes are the need.
  • Protocol changes are expensive. A new delegated-auth mechanism is months of work; a chain-of-delegation token format may be the long-term answer.
  • Existing clients should keep working. A moderator should not need a special app.
  • Standing should travel with the person, not be locked inside one app.
  • Who holds the credential? An app, a host, or nobody.
  • Attribution and revocation. Who did this, and how fast can they be stopped.
  • Portability. Can a community move, and does its arrangement move with it?

A · The app holds the account's session

callreplywhat the app does

Keeps

Nothing changes anywhere. Plain OAuth, one consent, honest about who granted what to whom: the account authorized the app. Works today with any PDS.

Costs

The app holds a credential for the account, with the app's scopes, not any one person's. Roles live inside the app and do not travel. Attribution exists only in the app's own records. Each app must be authorized separately by someone with the account's credentials.

B · Delegated scopes at the person's own server

callreplywhat a server does

Keeps

Standing travels with the person: one login, one session, and the app acts for whichever accounts allow it. A simple consent screen if the audience is left open.

Costs

A new protocol mechanism: a token that carries a delegation the account's PDS must verify, which is the kind of change that takes months and several implementations. Consent needs an audience picker the app cannot fill in. The account's own side, who allowed this person, still has to live somewhere.

C · Sign in as the account, at its own server

callreplywhat the host does

Keeps

Every existing OAuth client works, because it only sees an ordinary login. Consent is per account, at the account's server, with the client's own scopes. No protocol work. Runs today wherever the server implements it; a group host is this with the roles and a creation API built in.

Costs

The account must live on a server that implements the mechanism. Roles, the login method, the narrowing, and the log are that server's own, with no shared representation, so moving the account leaves its arrangement behind. In the group-host creation flow, the app ends up holding a credential for the community.

D1 · Delegated write

The person's own app, their own session. A service auth token from their PDS, repo set to the account. One login; the client must know how to address another account.

D2 · Sign in as the account

Option C's flow, with the policy read from the configuration instead of hooks. Any OAuth client, unchanged; the session narrowed, with act.sub.

D3 · Create from an app

The founder's app creates the account on any PDS with the founder's own service auth. No usable credentials; the founder is a controller. The app holds nothing.

Same bounds, same matcher the PDS already uses for OAuth scopes, same per-write log for all three.

D1 · Delegated write

callreplywhat the PDS doesrefusal

The app needs one thing from the person's PDS: an rpc: permission to mint service auth for the account's PDS. Naming that PDS as the audience keeps the consent bounded; a general-purpose client has to ask for an open audience, and the consent should say so in words.

D2 · Sign in as the account

callreplywhat the PDS does

The same shape as option C. The difference is where the answer to "may this person act, and how far" comes from: the account's configuration, which any PDS could read, rather than the server's own hooks and roles.

D2 · What a narrowed session gets

The client asked for

atproto repo:social.grain.group.item repo:app.bsky.feed.post?action=create blob:image/*

The account allows alice

repo:social.grain.group.item?action=create&action=delete

The token alice gets, as the account

atproto repo:social.grain.group.item?action=create&action=delete repo:app.bsky.feed.post?action=create blob:image/* act: { sub: did:plc:alice }

The intersection, applied at issue and again on every request against the account's current configuration. Remove alice and the session is refused on its next use and cannot be refreshed.

A controller additionally keeps account:delegates?action=manage, so the account's settings tool works for them. Nobody else's session keeps any account:, identity:, or rpc: scope.

D3 · Create a community from an app

callreplywhat the PDS does

Same steps as createAccount, minus credentials anyone could use. Operator policy (invites, allowlists, rate limits) applies as it does there. An account with no credentials of its own must keep at least one controller.

Keeps

The account can live on any PDS and move with its arrangement. One session or any client, the person's choice. Writes only. Attribution on the wire and in a log; revocation on the next request. No credential held by any app or host.

Costs

The reference PDS changes: a configuration, one auth path on writes, one login method, one claim, and a creation method. It is not deployable by one operator alone if the goal is "any PDS". For general-purpose clients on D1, the consent at the person's server is still an open audience.

Open questions

  • Discovery. How a person's client learns which accounts they may act for, without every PDS notifying every other. Every option shares this.
  • The open audience. Whether a general-purpose client's consent at the person's server can be made legible, or whether per-account consent at the account's server is the only honest form.
  • Calling out as the account. Whether a session as the account should ever be able to call a named service as the account, and how narrowly.
  • Portability. Whether the arrangement (who may act, and how far) should be a defined piece of host state that migrates, whichever option produces it.
  • Consent records. Keeping a delegate's consent to a client apart from the account's own.
  • Operator policy on creation. Who may create accounts this way, and how many.
  • The long view. Whether a chain-of-delegation token format eventually replaces any of this, and what should be built in the meantime.
